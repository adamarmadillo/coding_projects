LO - Understand the 11 systems of the body

Six levels of structural organisation in the human body
Useful for describing the form (anatomy) and function (physiology) of the human body
Chemical level is atoms and molecules
The cellular level is cells and organelles
The tissue level is tissues (groups of cells with certain properties) such as muscle or adipose
The organ level is individual organs such as the heart or lungs
The system level is groups of organs working to perform a specific task, such as the nervous system
The organismal level is the entire individual

Eleven body systems
The human body is organised into 11 systems:
Organs can be part of multiple systems, such as the pancreas
Integumentary system
This includes the epidermis, dermis, and hypodermis. 
The epidermis protects the inside of our body and is responsible for the production of vitamin D
The dermis contains hair follicles, sweat glands, nails, and sensory receptors. It is responsible for thermoregulation, protection, reinforcement (nails) and sensing touch, pressure, temperature and pain.
The hypodermis contains fat stores and is responsible for storing energy.
Muscular system
This includes skeletal muscles, tendons and aponeuroses
Skeletal muscles can be split into two groups, axial muscles and appendicular muscles
The primary function of skeletal muscles is to control skeletal movement, control “entrances and exits”, produce heat, support the skeleton, and protect softer tissues
Axial muscles provide support and position the axial skeleton
Appendicular muscles support and move limbs
Tendons are the fibrous, connections between muscles and bone
Aponeuroses are the sheet-like muscle to muscle connections, which sometimes connect to bone.
Skeletal system
This includes bones, cartilage, ligaments, joints and bone marrow.
Joints, ribs and various softer parts of the body consist of cartilage
There are two types of cartilage, hyaline (which is the primary type found in the body) and fibrocartilage (which makes up the discs in the spine and meniscus in the knee)
The bones of the body belong to two groups, the axial skeleton and the appendicular skeleton.
The axial skeleton protects the brain, spinal cord and thorax, while also supporting the weight of the body onto lower limbs.
The appendicular skeleton provides support and positioning for external limbs. It also allows muscles an anchor to move the axial skeleton.
Both consist of the surrounding bones, along with supporting ligaments and cartilages.
Ligaments are a fibrous connective tissue between two bones.
There are two types of bone marrow, red and yellow. 
Yellow marrow, found on the inside of longer bones, stores fat cells and is generally gained with aging.
Yellow marrow is converted to red after major blood loss
Red marrow, usually found in flat bones such as the pelvis and sternum, produces red blood cells, some is lost with aging.
Nervous system
The nervous system can be divided into the central nervous system (CNS), and the peripheral nervous system (PNS)
The CNS includes the brain and the spine, and is responsible for controlling other systems
The brain controls complex integrative activities which are either voluntary or involuntary
The spine performs less complex integrative activities such as reflexes
The PNS links the CNS with other systems and organs.
Senses are mostly detected by nerves in the PNS except for the optic nerve, which is part of the CNS
Endocrine system
The endocrine system consist of a variety of endocrine glands and organs that manage hormones throughout the body
Pineal gland controls day night rhythms with melatonin
Hypothalamus/pituitary gland controls growth, fluid balance, and other endocrine glands
Thyroid gland controls metabolism with thyroxine
Parathyroid gland controls calcium levels
Thymus controls maturation of lymphocytes (also called white blood cells, T-cells are also a type of lymphocytes)
Adrenal glands control water and mineral balance (aldosterone), tissue metabolism (cortisol) and cardio/respiration functionality (adrenaline)
Kidneys manage the production of red blood cells, and increase blood pressure and maintain calcium levels
Pancreas controls glucose with insulin and glucagon
Gonads control sexual characteristics and reproduction
Lymphatic system
The lymphatic system is responsible for the movement and filtering of fluids in the body, along with managing the immune system
It includes the lymphatic vessels, lymphatic fluid, B cells and T cells, lymph nodes (including tonsils), the spleen, and the thymus
Lymphatic vessels carry lymph fluid (also called lymph, which consists of a variety of excess fluids that are not reabsorbed into the capillaries, such as lipids, water, and protein
After blood plasma is filtered from the fluid found in the cells, the fluid is called interstitial fluid
Interstitial fluid is then absorbed into lymphatic capillaries and passed into afferent lymphatic vessels, which carry lymph into the lymph nodes
Lymph nodes then remove foreign substances through filtering, phagocytosis (engulfing pathogens), and immune reactions
then passing it on the efferent lymphatic vessels
Valves along the vessels ensure the liquid flows in one direction, which is then directed back into blood flow through the lymphatic ducts into the junction of the jugular and subclavian veins
The spleen monitors the circulation of blood cells, destroys pathogens with phagocytosis, recycles red blood cells, and produces immune responses
The thymus develops and maintains T cells
Cardiovascular system
This includes heart, blood and blood vessels
Blood carries (nutrients, oxygen, CO2, hormones, immune cells, HCO3-), removes waste, and regulates temperature
The blood vessels are divided into 3 groups
Arteries deliver blood to the capillaries
Capillaries diffuse interstitial fluid in and out of blood
Veins deliver blood back to the heart
The heart pumps blood and maintains blood pressure
Respiratory system
This includes the nasal cavity, paranasal sinuses, pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi, lungs, and diagram
This system consists of a linear path between organs to pass on material
The nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses are responsible for warming and humidifying air →
Pharynx conducts air → 
Larynx filters food and air into different ducts, controls the vocal cords, and conducts air → 
Trachea conducts air → 
Bonchi conducts air → 
Lungs exchange gases between the air and the blood, mostly including O2 and CO2
The diaphragm expands and collapses the lung in order to move air
Digestive system
This includes the oral cavity, salivary glands, pharynx, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, liver, gallbladder, pancreas, large intestine, and anus
Like the respiratory system, it is also a mostly linear system
The oral cavity breaks up food using the teeth and tongue, using salivary glands to act as a lubricant and begin digestion, and then conducts food and liquid down →
The pharynx diverts food away from the respiratory system and conducts food and liquid down →
The oesophagus conducts food down →
The stomach secrets acid, enzymes, and hormones, along with physically digesting food, which it then conducts down →
The small intestine secretes digestive enzymes, buffers acidity, releases hormones, and most importantly, absorbs nutrients
The liver regulates the amount of nutrients in the blood, and secretes bile (also called gall) which helps with the digestion of fatty acids and other nutrients
This bile is then concentrated by the gallbladder, and secreted into the beginning of the small intestinal tract
The pancreas also produces digestive enzymes, along with buffering acidity and secreting endocrine hormones
The remaining substance is conducted to the large intestine, which removes water, leaving the waste to be excreted by the anus
Urinary system
This includes the kidneys, ureters, bladder, and urethra
The kidney produces and concentrates urine, regulates pH and ions, manages blood volume and pressure, and assists in mineral balance and the production of blood cells
The urine is then passed into the ureters, into the bladder, and out the urethra, “conducting to exterior”

LO - describe epithelial tissue and its types along with its classification, location, structure, and function, describe the structure/function of the basement membrane, and understand the morphology of the exocrine and endocrine glands.

Epithelial tissue
Epithelial tissue covers the body surface, lines organs, and forms glands of the body
It can be a selective barrier, protect the body, and contains glands for secretions
Epithelium on the interior of lymphatic/blood vessels is called endothelium
It is arranged in sheets as single or multiple layers, which are held together by cell junctions
This includes tight junctions, adherens junctions, gap junctions, desmosomes, and hemidesmosomes
Cell junctions
Epithelial cells (epithelia) have 3 different surfaces, the apical, lateral, and the basal
Cell junctions are connections between the different surfaces of the cells
Most cell junctions connect from the lateral to lateral surfaces of epithelial cells except for hemidesmosomes, which connect the basal surface to the basement layer (if the cell contacts the basement layer)
Beneath the cell membrane and cytoplasm, microfilaments are found in bundles, linking the cytoplasm to the cell membrane and tying cells together
An example microfilament is actin
Intermediate filaments are found inside the cell, strengthening it
An example intermediate filament is keratin
Tight junctions
Tight junctions are made from individual sealing strands
They connect the cytoskeletons of adjacent cells
Mainly functions as a seal between the apical and lateral/basal surfaces of the cell
Prevents the flow of ions between the cells
Maintains the polarity of the cell, allowing the functions of both sides of the cell to match their environment
Many proteins involved but the most important ones are claudins and occludins
Adherens junctions
Also called belt desmosomes or adhesion belts (zonula adherens)
Long junction the wraps around the cell at a more basal position than tight junctions
Made of cadherins spanning between the cells, and catenins locking the cadherins through the cell membrane onto the actin microfilaments within the cell
Mainly functions as a strong structural bond as it powerfully interlocks cytoskeletons of cells
Less tight than tight junctions
Desmosomes
Dense button like plaque, only an isolated point of contact
Also spanned by cadherins, which is connected to keratin intermediate filaments by desmoplakin
Resistant to shearing forces
Hemidesmosomes
Desmosomes but specifically for connecting epithelia to the basement membrane (BM)
Uses integrin (instead of cadherin) to link keratin to proteins that connects to laminin in the BM
Gap junctions
Direct connection between cells
Made of 6 connexin molecules that creates a connexon (also called a hemichannel)
2 hemichannels create a gap junction, which allows up to ~1kDa small molecules
Basement membrane
All epithelia has a basement layer separating it from other tissues (almost always connective tissue)
Basement membrane has two parts:
Basal lamina
Secreted by epithelial cells, contains collagen, laminin, proteoglycans, glycoproteins, and other substances
Reticular lamina
Produced by the connective tissue beneath (called fibroblasts), contains collagen, fibronectin and other substances
Supports the epithelium, giving a surface for migration of epithelial cells when damage occurs (platelets and fibrin fills in while the epithelial cells move to cover)
Provides a physical barrier before reaching CT
Filters substances in the kidney
Epithelia does contain nerves, but does not contain blood vessels (avascular)
The exchange of nutrients and wastes relies on diffusion from blood vessels in  the connective tissue
Structure of epithelial tissue
There are two types of epithelia:
Covering/lining epithelia
Covers exposed surfaces and internal cavities
Includes the outer covering of the skin and some internal organs
Also includes inner lining of blood vessels, ducts, and cavities, including the interior of the respiratory, digestive, urinary, and reproductive systems
Covering and lining epithelia can be classified according to the arrangement or the shape of the cells
Arrangement of cells involves:
Simple, a single layer of cells, main functions are secretion, absorption, and filtration
Stratified, two or more layers of cells, mainly just protective
Pseudostratified, looks stratified, but all cells are in contact with the basement membrane, and not all cells are in contact with the apical surface
Shape of the cells are:
Squamous, which is flat and thin, mainly functions in diffusion
Cuboidal, which is about as tall as they are wide, main functions are secretion and absorption
Columnar, which is more tall than wide, main functions are also secretion and absorption
Transitional, required to be stratified, cells change from cuboidal to flat depending on organ shape, main function is to stretch, like in the urinary bladder
These shapes and arrangements can create a total of 8 combinations, only 5 of which we need to know the function of, along with 3 modificati
This include 
Glandular epithelia contains mostly secretory cells in arrangements to optimise for various purposes, it can be split into:
Exocrine glands, which secrete onto external surfaces
Endocrine glands, which secret hormones or precursors to interstitial fluid


Basement membrane consists of the basal lamina and the reticular lamina

LO - describe the general features of connective tissue, the general structure of compact bone, and some different types of connective tissue, along with their structure, location and function.

Connective tissue
The main purpose of connective tissue is to bind, support and strengthen other body tissues, but it is also a major transport system (blood) and energy storage system (adipose)
Connective tissue (CT) is, unlike epithelia, never found on body surfaces, and often highly vascular (except for in cartilage)
Like epithelia however it is also supplied by nerves (except for in cartilage)
Extracellular matrix
CT is made up of cells and the extracellular matrix (ECM)
The structure of the ECM can determine the qualities of the CT, such as in bone vs cartilage
ECM is composed of ground substance (GS), and protein fibres
GS is composed of water, proteins (including gelatin), and polysaccharides
Glycosaminoglycans
These polysaccharides are glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) (also mucopolysaccharides) and join with core proteins to make proteoglycans
GAGs are made of chains of repeating disaccharide units
Sulphated GAGs (most GAGs) bind to core proteins, however Unsulphated GAGs (hyaluronic acid) do not
GAGs have a variety of applications within the ground substance:
Hyaluronic acid is a viscous lubricating liquid which lubricates joints and also fills the eye
Hyaluronic acid attracts proteoglycans which attach to it through a glycoprotein link, creating complex structures that have various functions such as storing water or elasticity
Hyaluronidase makes GS less viscous, and is produced by WBC, sperm, and some bacteria
Chondroitin sulphate supports and provides various functions for bone, skin, cartilage and blood vessels
Keratan sulphate is found in bone, cartilage, and the cornea
Dermatan sulphate is found in skin, tendons, blood vessels, and heart valves
Protein Fibres
There are 3 different types of connective tissue fibers found in the ECM
Collagen fibres, which are thick, strong, but flexible to resist pulling forces
Most abundant protein in the body, features vary based on water content and other factors
Found mostly in bone, cartilage, tendons, and ligaments
Reticular fibres, composed of collagen in fine bundles with a coating of glycoprotein
Made by fibroblasts, also forms the basement membrane, and spreads across tissue
Elastic fibres, which are thinner than collagen and often consist of elastin surrounded by the glycoprotein fibrillin, which strengthens and stabilises it
Can be stretched 150%, found in skin, blood, and lungs
Fibroblasts
Migratory, and found widely distributed in CT
Function by secreting components of the ECM (fibres and GS)
Adipocytes
Found under skin and around organs, storing fat in the form of triglycerides
Macrophages
Mesenchymal Cells

Loose connective tissues
Areolar CT is the most common form of CT
It has all 3 type of fibres present (collagen, reticular, and elastic)
Adipose CT is dominantly made of adipocytes 
White adipocytes have a central triglyceride droplet
Brown adipocytes are responsible for producing heat
Dense regular CT
Makes up tendons, ligaments, and aponeuroses, mostly made of collagen fibre, meaning it takes long to heal
Supporting CT is cartilage
Osseous tissue (bones)
Compact bone (cortical bone) is on the outer layer of the bone, and is composed of many rod shaped units called osteons or haversian systems
Spongy bone (cancellous bone) lacks osteons and is porous on the inside of bones
Cortical bone is made of osteons, which have a calcified ECM as a result of mineralisation
Osteogenic cells are mesenchymal stem cells of the bone
They become osteoblasts, and begin laying down collagen, and start mineralisation
These finally become osteocytes which maintain bone tissue and exchange nutrients and wastes (they also have gap junctions)
Osteoclasts are large multinucleated cells, formed from the fusion of blood monocytes, and their purpose is to break down bone
Osteoclasts reabsorb dead bone
Chondroblasts lay down hyline cartilage callus
Osteoblasts lay down new bone
Osteoclasts remodel it

LO - describe the overall method of cell signalling, two major cell surface receptors, signal transduction via phosphorylation cascade, and the role of two main secondoary messengers, along with giving examples of cellular activities as outputs of cell signaling.

Cells need to produce and react to signals in order to function together
Local signals involves paracrine signalling and synaptic signalling
Paracrine signalling involves the secretion of a local regulator by a signaling cell which is detected by target cells
Synaptic signaling involves neurotransmitters which are received by the target cell
Long distance signalling involves the diffusal of hormones that travel to other parts of the body
Signals are recieved by a receptor, which relays the signal to a chain of relay molecules which eventually activate the cellular response, called reception, transduction, and response respectively
Relay molecules are often proteins that change state when they come in contact with their target molecules, this is often done by phosphorylation
These “activated proteins” cause functions to occur in the cell (response)
Receptors for water soluble molecules are bound to the cell membrane
Receptors for lipid soluble molecules are not bound to the membrane (as these hormones can diffuse directly into the cell anyways)
G-protien couples receptors (GPCRs) are transmembrane proteins that are bound to the membrane
They pass the plasma membrane 7 times, and have a variety of different ligands (target molecules to react with)
The couple with G-protiens which are essentially switches that turn “on or off” depending whether GDP or GTP is bound to it (ganosine triphosphate/diphosphate)
When GPCR connects with their ligand GPCR bind with the GPCR, replacing the GDP with GTP. G protein then dissociates from the GPCR and connects to the target enzyme, which then exhibits a cellular response while breaking GTP into GDP
Ligand gated ion channels/receptors involve a “gate” to an ion channel that can be activated by the ligand binding to the receptor enzyme
Signal transduction pathways are the chain of relay molecules that results in the intended cell response
This often involves breaking an ADP molecule in order to add a phosphate to a forceeding kinase (a relay molecule) which is called phosphorylation
This is called the phosphorylation cascade
Phosphatases are enzymes that remove the extra phosphate from the kinases, resetting the kinases to their original state
An example of the use of a rection like this is when a GPCR receives its ligand

Gene expression has 3 main steps:
Transcription
Transcription has 3 steps
Initiation is where the polymerase binds to the promoter (identifying gene sequence upstream for the polymerase to bond to)
The promoter contains a TATA box at the very beginning (sequence of T’s and A’s) which begins the binding of a series of proteins called transcription factors including TATA box binding protein (TBP), which acts as an initiation to for the polymerase
RNA polymerase II binds with the present and some new transcription factors to for, the initiation process
Elongation follows downstream through the gene, transcribing a strand of RNA
Termination occurs when the polymerase 
mRNA processing
Triplets of NTs code for the 20 different amino acids, along with a stop and go message
Ribosome builds the protein
The first MET tRNA bind to its respective triplets, the go message, holding a methionine
A GTP molecule is used to to attach the ribosome here, with the initial tRNA at the P site
tRNAs holding their amino acids will bond to their respective codon in the mRNA in the A site
This uses a GTP to increase accuracy and efficiency as there are many tRNAs to try
After the GTP is used the tRNA locks into the mRNA and bonds its peptide with the already formed chain, and the chain attaches to the new tRNA
Another GTP is used to move the new tRNA into the P site and kick the old tRNA out of the ribosome
Empty tRNAs are given a new aminoacid in the cytoplasm using aminoacyl-tRNA sythestases

Polypeptides have an amino end and a carboxyl end, which matches 5 → N and 3 → C

After the molecule is translated inside or out, there are still 100s of post translational modifications that may occur, such as phosphorylation, methylation, acetylation, biotinylation, carboxylation, carbohydrate addition, cleavage, and ubiquitination (combination of smaller proteins to larger ones)

LO: cell division

Cells in the body share the same dna, and can be programmed to grow/divide, live without dividing, or die
As they share the same DNA, different signals tell them to operate differently
Eukaryotic cell cycle
Somatic cells divide to produce a copy of itself
Not all cells divide, some are made to remain as-is
Most cells are in interphase
G1 or gap phase 1 is just after the cell has divided, where it duplicates its organelles
Most cells that don’t divide stop here
S or interphase is the duplication of all of its DNA
G2 or gap phase 2 checks everything and enters the mitotic phase where everything is prepared and replication of centrosomes is complete
In mitotic phase the centrosomes from a mitotic spindle
In prohpase the mitotic spindle gathers now condensed chromosomes
In metaphase the centromeres line them up on a plane, and anaphase begins separating the chromosomes onto each side
In telophase the cell begins to divide
Interphase dna replicates, prophase dna condenses into “sister” chromatids, metaphase chromosomes line up, anaphase sister chromatids separate before the nuclear envelope reforms in telophase
The result of this is 2 perfectly replicated daughter cells
There are checkpoints along the process
G1 checks if DNA is undamaged, cell size/nutrition is fine, and the right signals are present, if so continues cell cycle
If not the cell may exit to G0
M checkpoint checks if all chromosomes are attached to spindles, if so, continue to anaphase
Mitosis occurs in ovaries/testes
Produces gametes (with have a single set of 23 chromosomes)
Split into meiosis 1 and 2
In prophase 1, each respective chromosome, while bound to its sister chromosome, binds to their homologous non-sister chromatids (respective other chromosome)
This is call a tetrad, and DNA undergoes recombination
Centromere is the point where sister chromosomes cross
Random assortments of male and female parent chromosomes are separated to each pole adding more crossover
During mitosis 2, no DNA is replicated, and separation results in haploid cells
Genetic variation comes from independent assortment, crossing over, and fusion of 2 gametes

LO - Mutations and cancer

Most mutated proteins result from mutations in the coding parts of DNA
Mutations don’t always affect the structure and function of a protein as some DNA is redundant
Altered DNA is sometimes passed on to future progeny (resulting to a mutation in the entire individual), or just in a local/somatic cell, which does not affect the whole body
Large scale alterations are caused by chromosomal rearrangement when errors occur during crossing over
Small scale alterations are caused by the alteration of one or a few nucleotides
Small scale alterations can be caused by substitutions or insertions/deletions
Wild type genes are the original, non-mutated gene
Substitutions can be:
Silent mutations, which result in the same result with a different nucleotide effectively changing nothing, except for the shape of the RNA
Missense mutations causes the change of an amino acid and the effect can be estimated by the similarity of the resulting amino acid
Nonsense mutations occur when a stop is encoded, and most often cuts the protein too short to function
An insertion or deletion can cause a frameshift
Frameshifts are bad because if moved by 1 or 2 bases the entire gene is remixed to mean different things
3 nucleotide pair mutations are safer than 1 or 2 because they do not cause a frameshift
At the G2 checkpoint, a check is done for the maturation promoting factor (MPF)
This is created by cyclin, of which has a variable presence throughout the cell’s life, and a cyclin dependant kinase (Cdk)
A large network of interactions between proteins (called stop and go molecules) are involved at these checkpoints, which are required to stimulate or capable of inhibiting the continuation of cell maturation
Many factors of these rely on MPF
If stop or go molecules aren't working it can result in tumors/cancer
Over activation of proto-oncogenes (genes that stimulate cell proliferation), promote growth that results a tumor
Deactivation of tumor suppressor genes does not stop a cell from proliferation when the DNA is not in check, resulting in growth that may become a tumor

Amino acids consist of an amino (base) group, and a carboxyl (acid) group, along with their differentiating side chains
Each amino acid is held together by a peptide bond (R - COHN - R
The breaking of this bond is a hydrolysis reaction, and  formation of this bond is a dehydration reaction
Proteins have an end terminus on each end, called C and N, representing 3’ and 5’ respectively
The primary structure is an unfolded sequence of amino acids
The secondary structure involves the folding of polypeptides into alpha helices and beta sheets
Turns in the alpha helices are held by H bonds between the side chain of neighboring turns
Beta sheets are chains from the same polypeptide that face opposite directions, and are bonded by H bonds between their side chains
Beta sheets are often represented parallel arrows pointing in the direction of the sequence
Tertiary structure fold when the non-polar (hydrophobic) side chains interact with the aqueous environment and the interior of the protein (the hydrophobic effect)
Globular proteins have a condensed limited shape, whereas fibrous proteins are long spindles that extend longitudinally
Quaternary structures are made of multiple proteins formed together 
Enzymes work as catalysts by attracting and holding molecules in positions that increase the chance/rate of the intended reaction, and always end in the same state as they started afterwards
Electrostatic interaction also maintains protein structures, and are fairly strong, made of 2 of the 5 charged amino acids
Disulfide bonds are formed between cysteine when they dehydrate and form an S - S bond
Hydrophobic interactions cluster non polar sides away from water, and are reasonably weak but there are many of them
Denaturation occurs due to changes in ph, salt concentration, and temperature
Thermal irreversible denaturation occurs when new disulfide bonds are formed after the protein is unraveled
Changes in ions in water (pH) can interfere with electrostatic bonds
Salt concentration increases the chance of electrostatic bonds bonding to Cl or Na ions
Protein shape can be determined by X-ray crystallography, which grows a crystal made of the protein and measures the diffraction shape
Cryo electron microscopy and NMR are also useable

Describe the basic functions of a neuron
Neurons have a variety of features, such as dendrites, the cell body, axon hillock (initial segment), axon, and axon terminal
Neurons communicate with electrical and chemical signals, chemical signals are neurotransmitters
The dendrites receive electrical input

Basics of resting membrane potential (RMP)
RMP is the natural voltage potential between the inside and outside of a cell
The RMP of a cell is (usually) negative
Only neurons and muscle cells can suddenly change their RMP (this is called action potential)
RMP is measured using a ‘microelectrode’ which records the difference in charge
Patch clamps can measure the flow of ions in/out of a cell
RMP is a result of more negative charged ions inside the cell compared to outside (50-70mV)
Outside a neuron there is usually 10x (150:15mM) of sodium outside and 20x (100:5) of potassium inside the cell
This is because Na/K pumps have a ratio of 3 Na+ out to 2 K+ in
Non gated ‘leak’ channels are open at rest, allowing diffusion
Gated channels (voltage, ligand or mechanically gated) are closed at rest
There are many more leak K channels than leak Na channels (40:1 permeability)
Calculating equilibrium potentials for K+ and Na+ using the Nernst equation
Equilibrium potential is only for 1 ion type, and is the potential at which ions stop flowing according to electrochemical gradient
Essentially the point at which the electrical gradient and concentration gradient combined result in a net movement of 0

LO - action potentials

Neurons can communicate electrically with synaptic potentials (passive) and action potential
+ive ions moving out leaves negative charge inside = inside is negative voltage
Membrane potential in neurons is not constant (-65mV)
When it becomes more negative it is called hyper polarisation (moving away from equilibrium potential of Na (ENa)
When it becomes more positive it is called depolarisation (moving towards ENa)
Action potentials
Action potential is a fluctuation in membrane potential caused by a “transient” opening of a voltage gated ion channel
This change spreads along an axon like a wave
This action potential is triggered after the membrane potential reaches the threshold potential (~-55mV) (depolarised, closer to ENa)
The frequency of action potentials is carries information (neural language)
This allows fast and long distance signal transmissions between neurons (FM vs AM)
Three stages of the action potential and their associated changes in membrane permeability to Na+ and K+
At first a stimulus evokes a slow depolarisation from the RMP, e.g -70 mV slowly moves up to -55mV
Once the membrane potential reaches the threshold (-55mV), the voltage gated Na ion channels will open
This will cause fast depolarisation as Na+ ions flow in, reducing the difference, depolarising to ~+30mV
Repolarisation then occurs, passing RMP, which is then called after hyperpolarization (AHP), which will slowly depolarise to RMP
During fast depolarisation and repolarisation above the threshold (roughly) any extra stimulus will not retrigger the action potential (called absolute refractory period), however afterwards one may retrigger it, but will require a larger stimulus due to the bigger amplitude required to reach ~-55mV (called relative refractory period)
When threshold is reached, Na and K channels open, meaning PNa becomes larger (40:1 → 1:20) because PK of K channels is lower
This means that the MP increases towards +60mV for the equilibrium MP
The Na channels deactivate quickly, before the MP reaches +60mV
This leaves the K channels open, resulting in a decrease of the equilibrium MP below the RMP (Pk:PNa is now 100:1)
The MP goes below RMP temporarily before the K channels close and RMP equalises
Molecular events leading to Na+ channel activation and inactivation
The structure of an Na channel has a voltage sensor called the activation gate, and a ‘ball and chain’ called the inactivation gate
When the voltage sensor detects the voltage threshold, it opens letting ions through
Once the flow slows and becomes more +ive, the inactivation gate will be pushed in and block flow after ~1ms
After the voltage threshold is passed back up, the activation gate closes, followed by the inactivation gate opening
The amplitude of the stimulus can be graded, but the action potential cannot be graded, and is always 100mV
If the stimulus is larger, the only difference in the AP is the frequency it is reached (early voltage threshold is met immediately)
How action potentials can be generated by an electrical stimulus
AP can be evoked externally or internally
External activation is always experimental (using a battery/electrode) to create a current
Internal activation occurs when the passive current from post-synaptic potentials build up in the axon hillock, resulting in a AP
The current of a battery will originally flow on the outside of the cell, which does not trigger anything, but on the inside, some may flow stimulating AP
Current is the flow of positive charges, i.e the reverse of the electron flow
Current would flow from the + anode to the - cathode
Local depolarisation will occur where at the anode and hyperpolarisation at the cathode
If the local area of depolarisation reaches the threshold (reaching -55mV) it will trigger AP which then depolarises the next area after channels are opened
When externally activated, this wave of triggers will move bidirectionally (wave started in the middle)
In internal activation of an axon will be unidirectional as it starts at the first part of the axon hillock and cannot pass backwards through the cell body as the dendrites don’t have Na gated ion channels
Also because previous gates are in absolute refractory period
If Na channels are forcibly opened then the AP will stay maximised
Paralysis can be flaccid or spastic, flaccid being deactivated and spastic being constantly activated
Constant activation results is spastic paralysis

LO - synaptic transmission and neural integration

When neurons communicate the sender is presynaptic and receiver is postsynaptic
Motoneurons are like the presynaptic neuron and their synapse is called a neuromuscular junction (also called an end plate)
Neuromuscular junctions can be used as a model for synaptic transition
When the AP reaches the presynaptic terminal (synaptic knob), voltage gated Ca ion channels open and Ca flows in to the terminal
This will cause exocytosis or the release of neurotransmitters (acetylcholine or ACh) at the synaptic cleft
These transmitters will open ligand gated ion channels (both Na+ and K+, non-selective)
This will result in increase in charges and result in APs to move through the muscle cell called end plate potentials (EPP)
EPP is always suprathreshold, meaning a an AP is always triggered (as long as muscle is healthy)
Mechanisms of synaptic transmission in excitatory and inhibitory synapses
Chemical synapses in the CNS can be excitatory or inhibitory
Excitatory synapses cause depolarisation of the postsynaptic membrane, called the excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP)
Inhibitory synapses cause hyperpolarization of the postsynaptic membrane called the inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP)
EPSP neurotransmitters include ACh and glutamic acid (glutamate)
These transiently open Na+, K+ and sometimes Ca2+
K does not hyperpolarise because more Na will flow in that K
ISPS neurotransmitters include gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) or glycine
These usually open K channels and sometimes Cl-
Classes of neurotransmitters
There are 2 classifications of neurotransmitters:
Small molecule neurotransmitters are fast action (~milliseconds) and direct postsynaptic receptors
They include amino acids (glutamate, GABA, glycine), ACh, and amines (serotonin (5-HT), noradrenaline, dopamine)
Neuropeptides are much larger, they are slow (seconds-minutes), and a diffuse action
They indirectly act (metabotropic, meaning they cause a cascade that may modulate ion channels) on postsynaptic receptors or modulate the effects of other neurotransmitters
Many neuropeptides bind to GPCRs
Factors determining synaptic action of a neurotransmitter/modulator include:
The type of neurotransmitter/module
The type of receptor/channel complex in the postsynaptic membrane
The amount of receptor present in the postsynaptic membrane
Synaptic plasticity is the increase in receptors ^ sensitivity in the postsynaptic neuron as a result of repetitive stimulus
This is how memory works (long term potentiation)
Long term depression is the loss of memory, where a lack of or specific type of use reduces the number of receptors/increases resistance, decreasing sensitivity
An example of the factors contributing to synaptic action is glutamate
Glutamate can bind to AMPA, NMDA (which can also bind to glycine), and kainate receptors
Glutamate will produce responses that are fast in AMPA, slow in NMDA, and low amplitude but long in kainate
Neurotransmitters can be deactivated through diffusion, enzymatic degradation (e.g acetylcholine esterase), and re-uptake, using transporters in the presynaptic membrane (glutamate, dopamine, and serotonin transporters)
Neurons have thousands of synapses, some are inhibitory
Each synapse only creates around 0.1mV, meaning it would both dissipate and not trigger action potential
This can be solved using temporal or spatial summation
Temporal summation is many quick stimulations of the ESPS, which add on top of each other before the last dissipates, eventually creating a signal larger enough to reach threshold
Spatial summation is the triggering of many of the same synapses from (possibly multiple) neurons at the same time
Spatial summation can also be used by IPSP to essentially cancel out other synapses
Excitotoxicity
Excitotoxicity occurs when there is too much excitatory neurotransmitter (e.g glutamate)
This results in Calcium accumulation, resulting in an increase in reductive oxidative species, which leads to apoptosis as an enzyme kills the cell
The calcium accumulation can occur due to receptors remaining open due to excess Ca in the cell (called huntington's disease)
A proposed mechanism of schizophrenia is that neurons are lacking in GABA receptors (produce IPSP), meaning that signals aren’t inhibited, resulting in hypersensitivity in firing auditory neurons (hallucinating noises)
